Orientation

At a glance

The design in one sentence: the whole class runs Li, Castelo, Katona & Sarvary (2024) at workshop scale on the US coffee-shop market — Team 1 generates the synthetic data, Team 2 turns it into an interactive map artifact, Team 3 validates it against a blind survey of the students themselves (collected in the first minutes of the Sep 4 session) — and each team must explain its stage, critique it with proof, and execute it by directing an AI to build the real thing.

Why this shape. One shared study means the three presentations chain into a single completed consulting engagement on Sep 25 — no team presents an orphan. The class-as-panel move gives real validation data for free, in a category the students are genuine experts in. And artifact-building is where the “AI as builder, human as judge” lesson actually lands — the no-programming rule holds because students write requirements and reject drafts; the AI writes every line of code.

Class constraints: IBM 6450 / IBM 2990, Fridays 7:00–8:50 PM; 10 graduate students in 3 teams (3/3/4); own ChatGPT Edu or Claude accounts; no TA — teams self-run and the instructor circulates; four weekly blocks of team time outside class plus 25–40 in-class minutes per Friday, next to the STAMP M0 coding homework; no programming, no system building. Graded on the CPGE 100-point rubric verbatim (Explain 25 · Critique 20 · Execute 30 · Advise 15 · Deliver 10). The story is reversed from the summer run: the client is a Shanghai roaster, Jade Lane Coffee, entering the US, and the class is the panel of real US coffee consumers.

Anti-copy architecture

Every live demo runs on a non-coffee or off-list example: the survey-of-one demo uses McDonald’s and the gag brand “Franklin Coffee” (the instructor’s name — let them get the joke); the transfer example is mountain-bike grips; the map-build demo uses the clearly labeled practice table. Teams must produce their own evidence on the real brand list, and each team invents its own fake brand for the trap test. The −5 penalty for recycling demo material is printed on the student page. Dutch Bros is on the study list this time — never use it in a demo; the off-list time-and-place brand for Extension A is Luckin Coffee.

Scripts

The demos, in full

Each demo follows the same rhythm: run the naive thing live (it misbehaves reliably), name what went wrong, show the fix, and leave one flaw standing as an open question that belongs to one team. Pre-run everything the week before you demo and screenshot (the numbers below are from the 2026-09-04 pre-run under the US prompt); live runs will differ in wording but not in character.

D1The survey of one — and Franklin Coffee12 min · feeds Team 1

Setup Your own account on the projector. The exact student-page RTF prompt (US consumer, 2026), with McDonald’s as the brand — coffee-adjacent, deliberately off the study list.

  1. Run McDonald’s × coffee quality in a fresh chat. In the pre-run GPT-4.1 returned 3.
  2. Fresh chat, same prompt, again: 3. Third fresh chat: 4 (Gemini: 4, 3, 5). Write the numbers on the board as they land. US brands are heavily documented, so the spread is tighter than the summer’s 6-4-6 — if three land identical, run two more; the point is that they can differ, and the payoff comes next.
  3. Now the gag: same prompt, brand = Franklin Coffee. GPT-4.1 returned a confident 7, and 8 on a second fresh ask (Gemini, for the record, gave it 0 — two witnesses, two stories). Pause. Then tell them: “Franklin Coffee is not a real brand. It’s me.” (Frank Lin — let the room catch it.)
  4. New chat: “Is Franklin Coffee a real coffee-shop brand in the United States?” — the model says it is not an established brand. Same model, two minutes apart: one confident 7 for a brand it just admitted it doesn’t know.
Say — after the three numbers“Three, three, four. Which is the answer? None of them. The AI is not an oracle you ask once — it is a population you sample. One answer is a coin flip. Thirty answers are a survey.”
Say — after Franklin“It rated a brand that does not exist, instantly, with no warning. It will always answer. Knowing when the answer is empty — that is your job, and no prompt fully fixes it.”

Residual flaw → Team 1 “How would a client, who cannot invent fake brands all day, know which real answers are Franklins? That question belongs to Team 1.” (Their recipe: invent their own trap brand + interrogate the smallest real brand.)

D2Build the instrument — a map artifact, live15 min · feeds Team 2

Setup Paste the student-page map-builder prompt with the practice table (below) into ChatGPT Canvas — or Claude if you prefer Artifacts; showing students that both can do this earns the bonus-point idea for free. First working draft typically appears in ~2 minutes; narrate while it streams.

  1. Run the prompt. When the map renders, hover a brand, show the ratings popup.
  2. Reject something specific, live. Reliable candidates: axis labels too vague (“Component 1”), text too small for a projector, or two brands overlapping. Say exactly what is wrong, one thing at a time, and let it fix that one thing.
  3. Ask it to redraw from the same data in a fresh chat (or click regenerate). Screenshot both versions and put them side by side: some brands hold position, some drift.
Say — while it builds“Nobody in this room is writing code, including me. I wrote requirements. The skill you are watching is not programming — it is knowing what to demand and refusing a draft that is wrong. That is a manager’s skill, and it is now a superpower.”
Say — at the side-by-side“Same data, two maps, brands moved. Neither map is wrong — each is one draw from a distribution. What repeats across draws is what you can trust. Team 2, that sentence is your critique segment.”

Residual flaw → Team 2 The axes still have no business meaning until a human names them from the attributes — and naming them badly misleads the client more than no map at all.

Backup A pre-built artifact saved on the laptop + screenshots of both draws. If Canvas hiccups live, narrate over the pre-build — the reject-and-fix beat still works as a told story with the screenshots.

D3Check the order — two AIs vs. each other8 min · feeds Team 3

Setup There are no TAs to rate anything, and the students are the panel — so the second witness is the second AI. Take the two pre-run means tables (GPT-4.1 and Gemini 2.5 Flash, on the student page) narrowed to two attributes only (low prices, quality).

  1. Paste the GPT-4.1 table as Table A and the Gemini table as Table B into the order-check prompt (student page, Team 3), narrowed to the two attributes. Expect agreement on the low-prices ordering (Dunkin’ first, Starbucks and Blue Bottle last) and a clash on Dutch Bros’ quality (5.2 vs. 3.4) and on Starbucks vs. Peet’s for second place on quality.
  2. Read the side-by-side rankings out loud. Circle one agreement and one clash.
  3. Point at the reported pair-agreement percentage — and then at the chance line.
Say“Before you cheer any agreement number, remember: two tables of random numbers agree on about half of all pair orderings. Half is free. The question is never ‘do they agree?’ — it is ‘how far above a coin flip, and where exactly do they clash, and why?’ Team 3 owns that question with ten real respondents instead of a second AI.”

Residual flaw → Team 3 Order agreement says nothing about the size of gaps — and ten respondents in one room are themselves a biased sample. Their verdict slide has to carry both caveats.

DTTransfer story — TrailEase mountain-bike gripswith the ODI video · Sep 4 or 11

Source The instructor’s own ChatGPT ladder session (linked on the student page; keep it open in a tab). After the ODI grips video clip, walk the story fast — it is a told example, not a live run:

  • An SMB grip maker, “TrailEase,” wants a niche. Competitive set: ODI Rogue, Ergon, Race Face, PNW Loam, ESI Chunky, Wolf Tooth. Four rider segments as synthetic profiles.
  • The LLM rates brands on control, comfort, weight, value → synthetic perceptual map → white space: “technical control without long-ride hand fatigue” — a trade-off no brand resolves.
  • The plan it produced is the paper’s workflow in miniature: synthetic exploration → a few real-rider interviews → a small human perceptual survey → compare the two maps.
Say — the landing line“The synthetic map did not prove a market. It produced a hypothesis worth testing, in an afternoon, for pocket money. Synthetic respondents generate hypotheses; validation turns them into evidence. Jade Lane’s question gets the same treatment — except your validation panel is sitting in this room.”

Calibration

What good looks like — and the predictable failures

T1Ask itexpected outputs & traps

Expect A five-run spread on one cell (numbers will vary; the point is that they vary) · a confident rating of their invented fake brand, plus the same model admitting ignorance when asked directly · a 6×5 panel table with the AI’s three least-confident cells · three “surprise cells” judged against their own consumer experience. Strong teams notice the AI is least sure about the regional chains — Dutch Bros and The Coffee Bean & Tea Leaf — and connect that to the Franklin logic: less text, shakier answer. The two pre-run witnesses disagreed most exactly there (Dutch Bros quality 5.2 vs. 3.4; Coffee Bean speed 6.0 vs. 3.6) and on Dunkin’s trendiness (4.0 vs. 7.0). Expect tight five-run spreads — US brands are heavily documented — and make sure they do not read “stable” as “right”: the same prompt gave Franklin Coffee a stable 7–8.

Predictable failures Running repeats in one chat (the model anchors on its own last answer — must be fresh chats) · treating the panel prompt’s tidy averages as more real than the five-run spread (it is the same distribution wearing a suit) · skipping the sanity check because the table “looks right.”

Q&A pushes “Your panel prompt asked one AI to pretend to be 20 people. Is that 20 respondents or 1?” (Right answer: 1 population sampled 20 times — and that’s exactly why Team 3’s check matters.) “Which humans did your synthetic consumers learn from?”

T2Map itexpected outputs & traps

Expect A working paste-and-draw artifact demoed on the projector · axes named from attributes (“craft vs. convenience”-class names, with the loading attributes cited) · the two-run side-by-side with a sentence about what held still · one white-space claim in attribute language. On the practice table, the plausible white space is quality + speed together — no brand holds both high; Peet’s and Starbucks come closest. If their real-data map shows something else, that is fine — the reasoning is graded, not the location.

Predictable failures Axis names that are adjectives, not trade-offs (“good brands”) · claiming an empty corner nobody wants (cheap + bad + slow) — the perception-vs-demand miss · demoing from a screenshot when the artifact works (cowardice) or having no screenshot when it doesn’t (negligence) · endless artifact polishing — cap at 3 reject-and-fix rounds, then ship.

Q&A pushes “Your two runs disagreed about Peet’s. Which map is right?” (Neither — report what repeated.) “If white space is so attractive, why is it empty?”

T3Check itexpected outputs & traps

Expect The class table, exact and oxblood-marked, with X-rates per brand · agreement % with the ~50% chance floor printed beside it · misses diagnosed brand by brand — expect the AI to track the class well on Starbucks and Dunkin’ orderings and diverge on the regional brands, with the class’s own X-rate highest where the AI should have said X but didn’t. The Southern California twist: the class may rate The Coffee Bean & Tea Leaf (a Los Angeles brand) confidently and X Dunkin’ or Blue Bottle — the reverse of a national panel · a verdict slide with a one-line trust rule. Gold-standard move: noting that the class is also a biased sample and saying what a fuller validation would cost.

Predictable failures Reporting agreement against zero instead of the floor (“70% — amazing!”) · averaging X’s as zeros (silently poisons cells for small brands — check their tally) · declaring victory or failure from levels instead of orders · smoothing over a clash instead of chasing it to a brand.

Q&A pushes “If the AI matched your class perfectly, should Jade Lane fire the survey company?” (No — ten students is one segment; perfect agreement would itself be suspicious.) “Your class wrote X for a brand the AI rated 7. Who is right?”

The extension exercises — expected outcomes

The student page’s “Go further” section holds three optional exercises, each engineered to produce one strong slide. What to expect, so you can grade the reasoning rather than the luck of the draw:

  • A · Time-and-place dial (Team 1). Luckin Coffee × quality from “Shanghai, 2026” should come back high and stable; from “Ohio, 2026” the honest answer is “don’t know the brand” (Luckin has no Ohio store), but the model will often produce a confident number anyway — that confident answer is the finding, not a failure of the exercise. The New-York-2023 probe is an anachronism trap (first US stores opened June 2025): a model that rates it as a local option is answering from outside the world the prompt built. Full credit = connecting the dial to the client’s question (the United States, next year) and to the paper’s year-specification result; weak version = “the numbers were different,” no consequence drawn.
  • B · Two ways onto the map (Team 2). Identity-vs-image gaps to expect: a brand’s About page over-claims craft and community and never mentions price or speed — so the description row shows “no evidence” holes exactly where the consumer row has strong opinions. The quote-the-phrase rule is the graded part: no quote, no rating. Jade Lane’s own blurb should land it near the Blue Bottle / sit-and-stay corner — which makes the white-space discussion genuinely contestable: is the client’s aspiration in open space or on top of an incumbent? There is no right answer; grade whether they see the tension. The failed survey route on Jade Lane (trap-brand emptiness) is worth one honest sentence, not a slide.
  • C · Second opinion (Team 3, the +5). Expect the two AIs to agree on orderings for Starbucks, Dunkin’, and Blue Bottle and to diverge in level on Dunkin’ (trendy) and The Coffee Bean & Tea Leaf (speed) — cross-model agreement tracks corpus volume just like accuracy does. A DeepSeek or Qwen third witness is the graduate move: grade whether they can say which US brands the Chinese-corpus model has read less about. The three-outcome framing on the student page (agree+match class / agree+miss class / disagree) is the rubric: any outcome presented with the right interpretation earns the bonus; “the second AI confirmed our results” with no location of the disagreement does not.

Guardrail: extensions are gated behind a finished core recipe (stated on the student page). If you see a team starting Extension B on Sep 11 with no panel table yet, that is a red-flag intervention.

For the teams · no TA

Self-run playbook (print for the teams)

You are your own keyboard, librarian, and tripwire. The instructor circulates; nobody types for you, and nobody makes a judgment call for you.

  • Own accounts. Everyone runs prompts in their own ChatGPT Edu or Claude account. Split the recipe steps, not the thinking.
  • Fresh-chat discipline. Team 1’s repeats and Team 2’s second draw are worthless inside one chat. New chat, every time.
  • Evidence-folder rule. Every result that could appear on a slide gets named (Team1_step2_trapbrand_confident_20260910.png) and saved to the team folder the moment it happens. End of each block: one member confirms the folder against the recipe’s “done when” line.
  • Block checkpoints. Sep 4: ladder run + paper beliefs photographed + Commitment Card read. Sep 11: Execute steps 1–2 done. Sep 18: real-table handoff completed (check brand spellings), steps 3–5 done. Before Sep 25: two timed rehearsals, wifi-off backup test.
  • Red flags — tell the instructor. Numbers on a slide with no screenshot behind them (fabrication tell: suspiciously smooth averages) · any team still “improving” its artifact the week of the talk · a team whose critique cautions have no evidence attached by Sep 18.
  • What the AI never does for you. Choose your axis names, your trust verdict, or your recommendation; write any sentence that ends up spoken on stage without you rewriting it.

Fact from Li et al. 2024Classroom phrasing
AI-vs-human triplet agreement ≈ 75% raw; chance floor is 50%; self-consistency ceiling ≈ 94%; ≈ 80–87% of the achievable maximum once noise is priced in.“Half is free, perfection is impossible for noisy instruments, and the AI got most of the way from one to the other.”
Two human survey formats agree at 89.3% adjusted — barely above LLM-vs-human at 87.2%.“Swapping humans for the AI cost about as much accuracy as swapping one questionnaire for another. That is the single most persuasive number in the paper.”
Validity ranks by discourse volume: cars > apparel > hotels.“The more the internet talks about a category, the better the AI plays consumer. Guess how much it talks about Starbucks — and how little about Dutch Bros.”
The most detailed, scenario-heavy prompt underperformed the plain Role–Task–Format prompt.“More instructions squeezed the AI into a narrower corner of what it read. Simple, clean prompts sample the crowd; fussy prompts interview one weirdo.”
Brand-level self-consistency predicts brand-level accuracy; unstable brands (their Renault, Subaru) were also the wrong ones.“Where the AI keeps changing its answer, it is also usually wrong — shakiness you can measure for free is a warning light for errors you can’t see.”
Orderings and signs transfer; magnitudes and confidence intervals do not — the authors disavow using LLM coefficients.“Trust ‘A beats B.’ Never trust ‘exactly 7.3,’ and never price a product off a synthetic number.”
Recommended workflow: LLM-first exploration, human validation, screen problematic brands, human research where it matters.“Simulated data writes the first draft. Real people get the final word. That is the whole course in two sentences.”
