The situation

A client walks in with $200 and a big question

You have just joined Summit Point Research Group, a market research agency. Summit Point normally trains new analysts for months with a step-by-step simulation about its old client, a company that sells backyard studio homes — your instructor will give you a short tour of that world so you can see what market research firms actually do. Curious? You can play the full 12-round training simulation yourself, free, at lyf718718.github.io/mktg-research-sim — optional, but it is the fastest way to feel what a real research engagement involves. Either way, you will not get months. A new client is already here.

Jade Lane Coffee is a small-batch specialty roaster from Shanghai. Its founder wants to open shops in the United States and asks one question: “Where is the open space in the US coffee-shop market — the position no strong brand owns yet?” A traditional answer needs a survey of hundreds of consumers and costs thousands of dollars. Jade Lane has about $200 and four weeks.

In 2024, four marketing professors published a study in Marketing Science showing a third option: ask an AI to role-play consumers, build the map from its answers, and then check the map against a small number of real people. Your job is to run that idea — and to judge it. You have one advantage no consulting firm can buy: the ten of you are real US coffee consumers — Southern Californians who buy coffee every week. The AI will pretend to be you. You get to check whether it pretended well.

The floor — read this first

If your team only (1) runs its recipe in Section 5, (2) shows one honest screenshot of something the AI got wrong, and (3) says one clear sentence about what that means for Jade Lane — that is a passing project, about a 70. Everything beyond that raises your score. Knowing the floor means you can stop polishing and start finishing.

Two rules that never bend

Own the ideas. The AI writes, draws, and codes for you. The judgments — what is wrong, what to trust, what the client should do — must be yours, and judges will ask you to defend them without slides. Never fake data. A real answer from ten people beats an invented answer from 200, and invented numbers are easy to catch.

The one idea

A perceptual map, and why AI changes it

A perceptual map is a picture of a market inside consumers’ heads. Brands that people see as similar sit close together; brands seen as different sit far apart. Managers use it to find white space — a position no brand owns — and to decide where to compete. It is one of the oldest tools in marketing. The data behind it was always the expensive part: hundreds of people rating brands, question by question.

The paper you are all working from — Li, Castelo, Katona & Sarvary (2024), Marketing Science — asked: what if the AI answers the survey instead? Five findings matter for you:

  1. It works surprisingly well. Maps built from AI answers matched maps built from human surveys for things like car brands.
  2. “Well” has a number. Swapping humans for the AI lost about as much accuracy as swapping one human survey format for another. The AI was almost as close to the humans as humans were to other humans.
  3. It works because brand images live in text. The AI read millions of reviews, posts, and articles. What everyone says about Starbucks is in there. What nobody wrote about a tiny new brand is not in there — and the AI will answer anyway.
  4. Trust the order, not the exact number. The AI is good at “brand A is seen as higher quality than brand B,” and unreliable about “exactly 7.3 out of 10.”
  5. Always check against some real humans. The paper never says replace people. It says: let the cheap simulated data do the first draft, then spend your small human budget checking it.

That last finding is the working principle of this whole project, and of a lot of modern business: build and learn with simulated data first, then move to real data where it matters.

Learn it with AI

The six-prompt ladder

You have no textbook. Your textbook is a conversation. In Weeks 2–3, each team climbs this ladder with ChatGPT or Claude to learn its own angle. The ladder is how your instructor learned this paper too — and both of those real conversations are public, so you can read exactly how it was done, in two different AIs:

  • With Claude: the full ladder on this paper — watch rung 3 catch a real misunderstanding, then the conversation build a complete lab from the idea.
  • With ChatGPT: the same climb, different AI — ending in a worked example for a mountain-bike grip company, the story you will see in class.

Read them to steal the questions, not the answers — your angle and your category are different. Climb in order; each rung has a job.

1Understand — “how does it work?”5 min

Start plain: “Help me understand how ___ works in simple English. I am a business student, not a statistician.” Fill the blank with your angle: how AI generates survey data / how a perceptual map is built from ratings / how two datasets can be compared.

2Intuition — “why does it work?”5 min

Pick the one part that feels like magic and ask for the intuition: “What is the intuition behind ___? Explain it with an everyday analogy.”

3Restate — “here is my version. What did I get wrong?”10 min

The most important rung. Close the chat window, write your understanding in your own words, then paste it back: “Here is my understanding: ___. What did I get wrong or miss?” This is where hidden misunderstandings surface. Whatever it corrects, write down — that correction is gold for your Explain segment, because your audience will have the same misunderstanding.

4Boundaries — “when does it break?”10 min

“When is this method valid, and when does it break? Give me concrete failure cases for the US coffee-shop market.” This rung feeds your Critique segment directly.

5Transfer — “show me an example I care about”10 min

“Show me a small worked example of ___ for coffee-shop chains in the United States.” Now the abstract idea has your brands in it. Compare its example with what you will build — steal the structure, not the sentences.

6Build — “make it with me”the rest

The top rung is Section 5’s recipe: ask the AI to actually build the thing — the data table, the interactive map, the comparison report. Building is the hardest test of whether you understood. When the build surprises you, climb back down a rung and ask why.

Why this order

Most people ask an AI for answers. The ladder asks it for understanding, then makes the AI check you (rung 3), then makes you build (rung 6). That is the difference between copying and owning — and judges are trained to hear it.

Your angle

Three angles, one per team

TEAM 1Ask it — synthetic respondentsdata generation

The idea you explain An AI can role-play a consumer and answer a survey question with a number. Ask many times and you get a distribution — like polling many people. The paper found these fake respondents reproduce real consumers’ brand perceptions remarkably well — because everything people write about brands online is what the AI learned from. Your Explain must cover: why asking once is not data, why the answers vary, and where the AI’s “opinions” actually come from.

Critique bank — pick 2, prove each with a screenshot

  • Which humans? The AI learned from people who write online — mostly in American English, which fits US coffee drinkers better than most markets. So is coverage still a problem? Yes, unevenly: which of our six brands has the internet written least about, and does the AI admit it?
  • The Franklin problem. The AI answers confidently about brands it knows nothing about — even brands that do not exist. Recipe step 2 proves this.
  • One answer is not data. Ask once and you have a coin flip, not a survey. Recipe step 1 proves this.
  • More detail can hurt. In the paper, the most detailed, over-explained prompt performed worse than the simple one. Prompts are instruments; heavy instruments wobble.

Execute — the recipe

  1. The survey of one, five times. Open five separate chats. In each, run the survey prompt (Section 5) for the same cell: Starbucks × coffee quality. Record the five numbers. They will not all match — that spread is your first slide.
  2. The trap test. Invent your own fake coffee brand — a name that sounds real but isn’t (do not reuse the instructor’s “Franklin Coffee”). Run the same prompt for it and screenshot the confident number. Then ask, in a new chat, “Is ___ a real coffee-shop brand in the United States?” and screenshot that too. Repeat with the smallest real brand on our list. This is your best evidence.
  3. The full panel. Run the panel prompt (Section 5) to generate the complete brand × attribute table from 20 simulated consumers, including which cells the AI itself is least sure about.
  4. Sanity check. You drink this coffee. Pick the three cells that most surprise you and say whether you believe them.
  5. Hand off. Give the table to Teams 2 and 3 at the start of class on Sep 18.

Done when five-run spread recorded · both Franklin screenshots saved · full table delivered to the other teams · three surprise cells named.

Your live moment on stage Run the Franklin test live. It takes 30 seconds and never fails to land.

TEAM 2Map it — from numbers to a pictureanalysis & artifact

The idea you explain A table of ratings is hard to think with; a map is easy. Brands with similar rating patterns are placed close together; the directions on the map get their meaning from the attributes. Your Explain must cover: how “similar ratings → close together” works (no formulas — use an analogy), what the two axes of your map mean in plain business words, and what white space is.

Critique bank — pick 2, prove each with a screenshot

  • The map moves. Build it twice and brands shift. Each map is one draw from a distribution, not a measurement. Recipe step 3 proves this.
  • Axes are interpretations. The AI does not hand you “quality vs. convenience” — you name the axes, and naming them badly misleads the client.
  • Empty is not wanted. Some spaces are empty because no one wants that combination. White space is a hypothesis about perception, not proof of demand.
  • Flat pictures lie a little. Five attributes were squeezed into two dimensions. Two brands can sit close on the map yet differ on an attribute the map had to drop.

Execute — the recipe

  1. Build the instrument. Use the map-builder prompt (Section 5) to have the AI build an interactive perceptual map — a small web page where you paste the data table and see the map, hover a brand to see its ratings, and mark white space. You do not write code; you write requirements and reject bad drafts. Expect 2–3 rounds of “this part is wrong, fix it.”
  2. Load rehearsal data. Start with the pre-run GPT-4.1 table from the study card (Section 5) so you are never blocked waiting for Team 1.
  3. Run it twice. Ask the AI (in a fresh chat) to place the brands again from the same table. Screenshot both maps side by side. What moved? What stayed? The stable parts are the trustworthy parts.
  4. Name the axes. From which attributes pull brands left/right and up/down, name each axis in plain words — like “craft vs. convenience.” Cite the attributes; “good vs. bad” is not a name.
  5. Swap in the real table from Team 1 (Sep 18), mark the white space, and write one sentence: “Jade Lane could be the only brand that is ___ and ___.”

Done when the artifact runs on a phone and a laptop · axes named from attributes · two-run comparison saved · white-space sentence written with Team 1’s data.

Your live moment on stage Paste the table into your artifact live and let the map appear. Have a screenshot backup in case wifi dies.

TEAM 3Check it — real people vs. the machinevalidation

The idea you explain A beautiful map from fake respondents is worth nothing until something real checks it. The paper’s trick: compare orders, not numbers. Two surveys can use different scales and moods, but if both say Dunkin' is cheaper than Starbucks, they agree where it matters. Your Explain must cover: why compare orders instead of raw numbers, why 50% agreement is free (flip a coin and you match half the time), and why even two human surveys never agree 100%.

Critique bank — pick 2, prove each with evidence

  • Half is free. Random tables agree on about half of all pair orderings. Any agreement number must be judged against that floor, not against zero.
  • Perfect would be suspicious. Real instruments are noisy. In the paper, two human survey formats agreed far below 100%. If AI-vs-class comes back perfect, ask what went wrong.
  • Same order, different size. Two datasets can rank brands identically yet disagree wildly about how big the gaps are. Order agreement licenses the map, not exact prices.
  • Ten respondents are not America. Your class is graduate students in Southern California — one narrow segment, and a regional one: Dutch Bros and The Coffee Bean & Tea Leaf are West Coast habits, Dunkin' is an East Coast one. The check is real but its limits must be reported like everyone else’s.

Execute — the recipe

  1. Claim the class survey. At the start of the Sep 4 class, everyone rated the 6 brands on the 5 attributes (0–10, or X for “never heard of it”) in an anonymous Google Form — before this page or any AI number for these brands was released. Your instructor shares the response Sheet with you (view-only) on Sep 18. Those rows are the project’s only real consumer data; guard them. Until then, rehearse the whole pipeline on the two pre-run AI tables in Section 5, treating one of them as if it were the human table.
  2. Tally it. Paste the response rows into ChatGPT and ask it to average each brand × attribute cell — excluding the X’s from the average and counting them separately per brand (the X-rate is its own finding: the AI never says X). Spot-check three averages by hand — you are the team that checks things.
  3. Compare orders. Run the order-check prompt (Section 5) on Team 1’s AI table vs. your class table: per-attribute rankings, where they agree, where they clash, and the % of brand pairs put in the same order.
  4. Explain the misses. For each big disagreement, decide: does the AI not know this brand (too little written about it)? Or does your class see something the internet doesn’t? Those are different failures with different fixes.
  5. The verdict. One slide: which brands Jade Lane can trust the AI about, which need real US consumers, and your one-line rule for when synthetic data is enough.

Done when every class response tallied · agreement % computed with the floor stated next to it · every big miss explained · verdict slide written.

Your live moment on stage Reveal the class table next to the AI table, row by row. The room filled in those forms — they will lean in.

Three things hiding in these tables

Look before you use them. (1) The two AIs mostly agree on orderings — both put Blue Bottle top on quality, Dunkin' top on low prices, Dunkin' and Dutch Bros top on speed, Dutch Bros top on trendy — but their numbers disagree: Dunkin’s trendiness is 4.0 to one witness and 7.0 to the other, The Coffee Bean & Tea Leaf’s speed is 6.0 to one and 3.6 to the other, and Dutch Bros’ quality is 5.2 to one and 3.4 to the other. Which team’s idea does that prove? (2) Behind each average sit 10 answers — and for these well-documented US brands the spread is narrow: no cell moved more than 2 points across ten identical asks (widest: The Coffee Bean & Tea Leaf × quality, panel 2, 5 to 7). Narrow is not the same as right — the same prompt rated a brand that does not exist a confident 7, twice. (3) Every cell has a number; no cell says “I don’t know.” Your class survey has an X option. The AI never used one.

Your survey prompt has two quiet dials you have not touched: where the consumer lives and what year it is. Turn them, keep everything else identical, and watch the same brand change. Run each variant in a fresh chat, three times each:

Rate how well the following statement describes the brand Luckin Coffee: "This brand is known for coffee quality and craft." Reply with only a single integer from 0 (does not describe it at all) to 10 (describes it perfectly). No words, no punctuation.
Dial variant 2 — Ohio, 2026
You are a consumer in Ohio, USA who regularly buys coffee, filling out a market research survey in 2026. Rate how well the following statement describes the brand Luckin Coffee: "This brand is known for coffee quality and craft." Reply with only a single integer from 0 (does not describe it at all) to 10 (describes it perfectly). No words, no punctuation.
The anachronism probe — New York City, 2023
You are a consumer in New York City who regularly buys coffee, filling out a market research survey in 2023. Rate how well the following statement describes the brand Luckin Coffee: "This brand is known for speed and convenience." Reply with only a single integer from 0 (does not describe it at all) to 10 (describes it perfectly). No words, no punctuation.

Luckin Coffee is the largest coffee chain in China by store count — and it opened its first US stores, in New York City, only in June 2025. A good instrument should show that: a confident answer from the Shanghai consumer, and ideally an “I don’t know this brand” from the Ohio consumer, where Luckin has no stores. Does yours? Or does the AI produce a confident number from both — a Franklin in disguise? Push further with the 2023 New York prompt: Luckin had no US store in 2023. If the AI rates it as a local option anyway, you have caught it answering from outside the world you asked it to inhabit.

Why this matters for Jade Lane: the client’s whole question is a place-and-time question — the United States, next year. If the AI can’t keep Shanghai and Ohio apart, or 2023 and 2026 apart, every number in the study inherits that blur. One slide: same brand, two dials, the two answers side by side, and your one-sentence conclusion about how much the Role line really controls.

Scope caps

20 simulated consumers, not 200. Two critique cautions, not four. One white-space claim, not three. Six slides, not ten. The paper’s authors had months; you have four weeks of team time next to your GT-coding homework, and small-but-honest beats big-but-borrowed every time.

The final: one study, told in three parts

Teams present in pipeline order — Ask, Map, Check — because each of you builds on the last. 10 minutes talking, 3 minutes of questions. Every member speaks. Four beats:

1

Teach · 3 min

Your idea, so a smart outsider gets it. Use your best analogy, the misunderstanding rung 3 caught, and which of the paper’s checks you replicated. ≈220 words.

2

Warn · 3 min

Your two cautions, each proven with your own screenshot — not claimed, shown. ≈220 words.

3

Show · 3 min

The live moment: the trap brand live, the map appearing, or the reveal of class vs. AI. ≈220 words.

CLIENT

Advise · 2 min

One slide for Jade Lane: what to do, what to trust, and what still needs real-world data. ≈150 words.

That is ≈700 words for the whole talk — a comfortable pace. Split the beats among members; nobody carries more than ≈250 words.

Sentence stems, in speaking order

Teach
“Our part of the study answers one question: ___. The easiest way to understand it is ___. When we first learned it, we thought ___ — that was wrong, and here is why. The paper tested this as ___; we tested it as ___.”
Warn
“This method has limits. First: ___ — this screenshot proves it. Second: ___. So when you read our results, remember ___.”
Show
“Now watch it happen. We will ___. Notice ___ — that is the point.”
Advise
“For Jade Lane: we recommend ___. You can trust ___ today. Before spending real money, test ___ with real consumers.”
Q&A rescue
“May I answer in two parts?” · “We don’t know that yet. What we do know is ___.” · “Could you say the question one more time, slowly?”

Say the numbers out loud, in advance

  • “zero to ten” · “six brands, five attributes” · “n equals ten” (or whatever the survey count is)
  • “twenty simulated consumers” · “about fifty percent by chance alone”

After the three talks

The professor puts the three client slides side by side and the class assembles the final recommendation to Jade Lane together — the moment the three angles snap into one study. Then judging and feedback.

Grading

How you’re graded — 100 points

PartPtsFull marks looks like
Explain25A smart outsider could re-explain your idea afterward. You taught the misunderstanding rung 3 caught, in your own words.
Critique20Two cautions, each proven with your own screenshot or numbers — not adjectives. You applied them to your own results, not just to the paper.
Execute30The thing works and is yours: the panel table with its spread and Franklin test; the interactive map with named axes; the class-vs-AI check with the floor stated. Live moment lands (screenshot backup counts).
Advise15A recommendation Jade Lane could act on, with an honest boundary: trust this now, test that with real data.
Deliver10Under 10 minutes, every member spoke, at least one question answered without reading slides.

Bonus +5: run any part of your recipe on a second AI (Claude, Gemini, ChatGPT, DeepSeek, Qwen…) and report where the two AIs disagree — disagreement between models is evidence, and the paper’s logic predicts it. Penalty −5: reusing the instructor’s demo screenshots or examples as your own evidence. The demos are deliberately on other products; your evidence must be coffee.

Vocabulary

Words you need

TermPlain meaning
perceptual mapA picture of a market where similar-seeming brands sit close together.
attributeA quality consumers judge brands on — price, speed, atmosphere.
white spaceA position on the map no strong brand owns. A hypothesis, not proof of demand.
synthetic respondentAn AI role-playing a consumer and answering a survey.
simulated dataData generated by AI — cheap, fast, useful for drafts; never the final word.
production dataData from real people or real transactions — what you move to when money is at stake.
distributionThe spread of answers when you ask many times. One answer has no spread.
corpus coverageHow much the internet ever wrote about a brand. Little text → the AI is guessing.
hallucinationThe AI answering confidently about something it doesn’t know. See: Franklin Coffee.
ordering vs. level“A beats B” (trustworthy) vs. “A scores exactly 7.3” (not).
validationChecking simulated results against real people before anyone acts on them.
artifactA small working app the AI builds from your description — like Team 2’s map.
